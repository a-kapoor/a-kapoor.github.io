What's happening? The colour map shows a bowl-shaped loss function L(w₁, w₂) = w₁² + w₂². The ball follows the negative gradient (steepest downhill direction) at each step. A smaller learning rate takes cautious steps; a larger one leaps — and may overshoot!
∞
From Loss Surfaces to Neural Networks

This demo shows 2D optimization on a bowl. Real models have millions or billions of parameters — too many to visualize. But the core idea is identical: compute the gradient (direction of steepest ascent in error), step downhill, repeat. Every neural network training loop — from tiny MLPs to GPT-4 — is just this same algorithm applied to a vastly higher-dimensional space. The bigger the network and dataset, the more careful you must be with learning rates and batching.

From 2D Classification to Billion-Dimensional Text

This SVM works in 2D, so we can see the decision boundary clearly. But text classification (spam detection, sentiment analysis) happens in 10,000+ dimensional embedding spaces — one per word in a learned vocabulary. The same principle applies: find a hyperplane that maximizes the margin between classes. Modern LLMs use much richer classifiers, but SVMs remain powerful for structured tabular data and as building blocks in larger systems.

K-Means Clustering

The algorithm groups scattered points into colour-coded clusters.

Running…
What's happening? K-Means alternates two steps: (1) Assign — colour each point by its nearest centroid; (2) Update — move each centroid to the average of its points. Repeating converges to stable clusters. The coloured background shows the Voronoi regions — every pixel is painted by its nearest centroid.
∞
From Toy Clusters to Customer Segmentation

K-Means here groups 100 dots by 3–5 categories. In the real world, companies segment millions of customers by behavior (purchase history, browsing patterns, geography) across hundreds of dimensions. K-Means still works, but you pay computational costs and must decide K blindly. Modern alternatives like DBSCAN and Gaussian Mixture Models handle density better, and deep learning embeddings replace hand-crafted features. The assign-then-update rhythm, though, is timeless.

Vector Embeddings

Words become points in 3-D space — similar meanings land close together.

Drag to rotate the 3-D view. Use the zoom slider or mouse wheel to zoom. Hover or tap a word to inspect it, then select two words to compare.
What's happening? Each word is encoded as a short vector of numbers (here shown in 3-D for visualisation). Two words that are semantically similar (e.g. "cat" & "dog") get vectors that point in nearly the same direction, so they appear close together. The cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors: 1 = identical direction, 0 = unrelated. This is the core idea behind word2vec, GloVe, and modern LLM embeddings.
∞
From 3-D Toys to 1,536-D Real Embeddings

This demo shows 3D embeddings for visualization. GPT-3 uses 12,288-dimensional embeddings; modern systems go even higher. You cannot visualize these spaces directly, but the mathematics remains identical: each word or concept is a point in high-dimensional space, and cosine similarity measures alignment. Embeddings are the secret sauce of modern NLP — they compress meaning into dense vectors that models can learn and compare at scale. Everything from semantic search to recommendation systems relies on this idea.

🧠 The Transformer — Zero to Hero
1
Embedding — Words Become Numbers

A model can't read "cat" — but it can crunch [0.92, 0.08, 0.10, …]. An embedding table maps every word to a unique vector. Similar words land nearby: "cat" and "dog" almost overlap, while "cat" and "on" are far apart.

💡 Analogy: GPS coordinates for meaning. "Paris" and "Lyon" are neighbours; "Paris" and "tuna" are continents apart.
2
Positional Encoding — "Where Am I?"

Without position info, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look identical. Positional encoding injects order by adding sine/cosine wave patterns at each slot, so the model knows where each word sits.

xᵢ = embedding(wordᵢ) + PE(positionᵢ)
💡 Analogy: Seat numbers at a dinner table. The script doesn't change, but the actor in Chair 1 has a very different role from the actor in Chair 5.
3
Q, K, V — Three Roles for Every Token

Each token is projected through three learned weight matrices:

  • Query (Q) — "What am I looking for?" The verb asks: "Where's my subject?"
  • Key (K) — "What do I advertise?" The determiner says: "I introduce a noun."
  • Value (V) — "What info do I carry?" The actual content that flows forward.
Q = x · WQ   K = x · WK   V = x · WV
💡 Analogy: At a party, Q is your question, K is name-tags other guests wear, and V is the conversation they share once you pick who to talk to.
4
Attention — "Who Matters Most?"

Each token compares its Query against every earlier token's Key (dot product, scaled by √d). Softmax turns scores into weights summing to 1. The output is a weighted blend of everyone's Values.

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(Q · KT / √d) · V

A causal mask prevents peeking at the future — each position only attends to earlier ones. This is what makes it autoregressive: one word at a time, left to right.

💡 Analogy: A round-table conversation where "sat" perks up when it hears "cat" — because a verb cares about its subject. The mask is a rule: you can only hear people who spoke before you.
5
FFN — Think It Over

After attention mixes tokens, each position runs through a feed-forward network (two linear layers with ReLU). This is where the model "digests" context — combining features, spotting patterns, preparing to predict. A residual connection (output = FFN(x) + x) ensures nothing is lost.

FFN(x) = ReLU(x · W1 + b1) · W2 + b2
💡 Analogy: After gathering opinions from the table (attention), you sit quietly and digest what you heard. The FFN is that quiet moment of reflection before you speak.
6
Prediction — The Final Vote

The last position's hidden state is multiplied by an output projection matrix to score every word in the vocabulary. Softmax converts scores to probabilities. The highest-probability word wins.

logits = h · Wout  →  P(next word) = softmax(logits)
💡 Analogy: Election day. Every vocab word is a candidate. The context vector casts weighted votes, and softmax tallies them into a probability distribution.

From 2→4→1 to Billion-Parameter Deep Nets

This 2→4→1 network is just a tiny MLP solving a toy problem. Real deep learning stacks hundreds or thousands of layers, each with thousands of neurons. ResNet-50 has 50 layers and 25 million parameters. GPT-4 has ~170 billion. The core loop is identical: apply linear transformation + nonlinearity, repeat, backprop gradients. What changes is scale: more layers mean the network can learn deeper hierarchies of pattern — from pixels to edges to shapes to concepts.

Overfitting

Drag the slider to increase polynomial degree and watch the model overfit.

What's happening? We fit a polynomial of increasing degree to noisy data. Low degree = underfitting (too simple). High degree = overfitting (memorises noise, wiggles wildly). The right panel shows train vs test error — the classic U-shaped test curve.
∞
From Toy Curves to Real-World Generalization

This demo shows overfitting on synthetic data where you control the noise level. Real datasets — medical images, financial data, language — are messier and deceptively complex. The bias-variance tradeoff never disappears: too simple a model misses structure; too complex a model learns spurious correlations. Modern solutions include regularization (L1/L2 penalties), dropout, early stopping, data augmentation, and ensemble methods. Even large models with billions of parameters can overfit if your test set is small or unrepresentative.

Convolution

Apply a filter kernel to an image and see the output feature map.

What's happening? A 3×3 kernel slides across the input image. At each position, we multiply element-wise and sum — that's the convolution. Different kernels extract different features: edges, blur, sharpening. CNNs learn the best kernels for the task.
∞
From Hand-Crafted Filters to Deep Vision

This demo manually applies edge/blur/sharpen filters. Real CNNs like ResNet or YOLOv8 stack hundreds of layers, each learning its own filters through gradient descent. Early layers learn simple textures; middle layers combine them into shapes; deep layers recognize objects and scenes. The same convolution operation scales from detecting edges in 28×28 digits (MNIST) to recognizing objects in 4K images. The locality property — each neuron sees only a small neighborhood — is what makes convolution efficient on large images where fully-connected networks would be prohibitively expensive.

PCA

See how principal components capture the directions of maximum variance.

What's happening? PCA finds the axes (principal components) along which the data varies the most. PC1 captures the most variance; PC2 the next most. The right panel shows how much variance each component explains. Projecting onto PC1 alone reduces 2-D data to 1-D while keeping maximum information.
∞
From 2-D Toy Data to Compressing Gene Expression

This toy example reduces 2D down to 1D. Real PCA tackles massive datasets: genomics researchers use it to compress 20,000+ gene-expression measurements per patient into interpretable 2D scatter plots. Face recognition systems use PCA (eigenfaces) to reduce 200×200 pixel images to vectors of just 50 dimensions while keeping recognition accuracy high. Modern alternatives like t-SNE and UMAP handle non-linear structure better. But PCA remains a workhorse: it is fast, interpretable, and often a crucial first step in exploratory data analysis.

An agent learns to navigate a grid world to reach the goal.

Training…
What's happening? The agent uses Q-learning: it maintains a value for each (state, action) pair. When it reaches the goal (+1 reward) or wall (−0.1), it updates values using the Bellman equation. Over time the arrows converge to the optimal policy. The heatmap shows cell values.
∞
From Grid Mazes to Autonomous Vehicles & Game-Playing AI

This Q-learning agent lives in a tiny 5×5 grid. The same reinforcement learning algorithm powers AlphaGo (which defeated world chess and Go champions), robot manipulation (learning to grasp objects), and self-driving cars (learning safe navigation policies). As state spaces grow (from 25 cells to billions of possible game positions), tabular Q-learning becomes infeasible — you must combine it with deep learning (Deep Q-Networks, DQN). The reward signal is also crucial: a simpler reward function generalizes better and is easier to align with human intent than a complex one.

Decision Tree

Watch a tree recursively split the feature space to classify points.

What's happening? At each node the tree picks the feature and threshold that best separates the classes (maximum information gain / Gini reduction). Each split adds an axis-aligned boundary. More depth = more splits = finer regions — but risk overfitting with too many.
∞
From Single Trees to Random Forests & Gradient Boosting

One decision tree is interpretable but often biased (high variance on noisy data). A Random Forest trains hundreds of trees on random subsets and averages their predictions — this ensemble approach typically beats a single deep tree. Gradient Boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM) does something cleverer: it trains trees sequentially, each one correcting the errors of the previous ones. These boosted models often outperform neural networks on tabular data (the kind in spreadsheets). The tradeoff: you lose the single tree's interpretability, but gain predictive power and robustness.

Backpropagation

Watch gradients flow backward through a network using the chain rule.

Press ▶ to run one forward+backward pass.
What's happening? Backprop computes ∂Loss/∂w for every weight via the chain rule. Green arrows = forward pass (compute output); red arrows = backward pass (compute gradients). Each weight is updated: w ← w − η·∂L/∂w. This is how all neural networks learn.
∞
From Toy Geometry to Training Trillion-Parameter Models

Backpropagation is the engine of deep learning. This toy network has three weights. GPT-4 has ~170 billion. Backprop computes gradients for all of them in a single backward pass. The chain rule composes perfectly: ∂L/∂w₁ depends on activations and weights downstream, and the backward pass propagates all these dependencies efficiently. Modern systems use variants: gradient accumulation (for larger batches), mixed precision (train in lower precision to save memory), and distributed backprop (split the computation across hundreds of devices). The core idea — gradient descent via the chain rule — remains unchanged.
